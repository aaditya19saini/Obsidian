# Question: 
_"First, I wouldn't jump to conclusions. I'd start by understanding the WHY — is it a skill issue, a motivation issue, or a personal issue? Because each one needs a different approach._

_My first step would be a **one-on-one conversation** — not a confrontation, but a genuine check-in. I'd ask how they're doing, if there's anything blocking them, and share specific observations about their performance — not vague feedback, but concrete examples, like 'I noticed your output has dropped this week' or 'you've been missing these targets.'_

_If it's a **skill gap**, I'd arrange additional training or pair them with a stronger performer for a few shifts so they can learn by doing._

_If it's a **motivation issue**, I'd try to understand what's causing it — maybe they feel unrecognized, or they're stuck in a monotonous task. I'd look at whether I can rotate their responsibilities, set smaller achievable goals, and recognize their progress._

_If it's a **personal issue**, I'd be empathetic and see if there's flexibility I can offer in the short term, while keeping expectations clear._

_Throughout this, I'd **document the conversations and set clear, measurable expectations** with a timeline — so both of us know what 'better' looks like. I'd follow up regularly, not just once._

_If despite all this support the performance doesn't improve, I'd escalate it through the proper HR process — because at the end of the day, one person's consistent underperformance affects the entire team's output and morale."_


# Sales internship
### **S — Situation** _(15 sec)_

> _"During my internship at Assiz and Sarah Healthcare, I was assigned to LungFit PH — a life-saving medical device that generates nitric oxide from the atmosphere for neonatal critical care. The product was globally proven but brand new to the Indian market. We faced two major challenges: the price point was significantly higher than competitors, and competing products already had widespread installations across hospitals. Doctors didn't know the brand, and hospital budgets are locked through annual CAPEX — so convincing anyone to even consider a new high-value device was extremely tough."_

---

### **T — Task** _(5 sec)_

> _"My goal was to break into the Delhi-NCR market — introduce LungFit to as many hospitals as possible, build a pipeline of interested neonatologists, and set up product demos."_

---

### **A — Action** _(45 sec)_

> _"I started with research. I built detailed competitor comparison sheets — highlighting where LungFit was superior in reliability, ease of use, and patient outcomes. I wanted to walk into every meeting armed with data, not just a sales pitch._
> 
> _Then I was strategic about targeting. Instead of cold-calling every hospital, I identified progressive doctors — the ones known for adopting new technologies early. If I could win an early adopter, they'd influence others._
> 
> _I personally trained myself to operate and maintain the device. So when a doctor asked a technical question, I could answer on the spot — that immediately built credibility. I wasn't just an intern reading a brochure._
> 
> _I visited 19 hospitals across Delhi-NCR — 10 government, 9 private. In every meeting, I led with the doctor's problems — what are your current challenges in neonatal care? What frustrates you about existing solutions? Then I positioned LungFit as a solution to their specific pain points._
> 
> _I also provided global case studies and installation lists to every doctor, and followed up systematically after each visit."_

---

### **R — Result** _(15 sec)_

> _"We didn't close a sale during my internship — this industry has 12–18 month sales cycles, and budgets were already committed. But I built a qualified pipeline of over 15 doctors who were genuinely interested, with multiple demo sessions booked. I took the brand from zero awareness to active consideration in 19 hospitals in just 3 months._
> 
> _The biggest takeaway for me was that in high-stakes environments, results come from building trust and credibility one conversation at a time — and that patience and persistence are just as important as speed."_
