### I. Dehydration and Drying Technologies

**Fundamental Principles**

- **Dehydration vs. Drying:** **Dehydration** is the highly controlled process of removing moisture from food to an almost bone-dry condition to extend shelf life. **Drying** refers to removing water to reach an equilibrium moisture content, often under uncontrolled climatic conditions (like sun drying).
- **Purpose:** The main goal is to lower the water activity ($a_w$ to between 0.2–0.6) to inhibit the growth of microorganisms (bacteria, molds, yeasts) and stall enzymatic and chemical reactions.
- **The Drying Process:** Drying involves simultaneous heat and moisture transfer. The process features a **constant-rate period** (where free moisture evaporates from the surface) and a **falling-rate period** (where internal moisture slowly diffuses to the surface) [Diagram of dehydration curve: 107].

**Pre-treatment Methods** Before drying, foods often undergo pre-treatments:

- **Blanching:** Partial pre-cooking in boiling water to inactivate natural enzymes, remove hardness, and reduce bacterial load.
- **Sulphuring:** Exposing food to sulphur fumes to check mold growth, prevent oxidation/browning, and act as a preservative.

**Drying Methods and Equipment**

- **Kiln Drying:** A simple convection dryer where hot air rises through a slotted floor holding the food. Widely used for apples [Diagram of Kiln Dryer: 110].
- **Cabinet/Tray Dryer:** Food is placed on trays in a cabinet, and hot air is circulated.
- **Tunnel Dryer:** Trays on trolleys move continuously through a long heated tunnel [Diagrams of Tunnel Dryers: 33, 47, 110].
- **Conveyor/Belt Dryer:** Food is conveyed on a mesh belt while hot air is forced through the bed [Diagrams of Conveyor Dryer: 46].
- **Drum Drying (Roller Drying):** Used for liquids or pastes (like mashed potatoes or milk). The food is applied in a thin layer to a rotating drum heated internally by steam (100–150°C). The dried film is scraped off by a blade.
- **Spray Drying:** Liquid food is atomized into minute droplets and sprayed into a stream of hot air (around 200°C), drying into powder in seconds. Ideal for milk, coffee, and juices [Diagrams of Spray Dryers: 111, 177].
- **Foam Mat Drying:** A foaming agent (e.g., glycerol, egg albumin) is added to a liquid concentrate. It is whipped into a foam and dried. The porous structure drastically increases the surface area, making drying very fast.
- **Freeze Drying (Lyophilization):** Used for highly heat-sensitive foods. The food is frozen, and then a vacuum is applied. The ice **sublimes** directly into vapor without becoming liquid. This occurs below the "triple point" of water [Phase diagram of water and Freeze Dryer diagram: 111, 171, 172, 173].
- **Fluidized Bed Drying:** Hot air is blasted upward through a mesh belt, suspending the food particles in the air so they act like a boiling liquid, allowing for highly uniform drying [Diagrams of Fluidized Bed: 52, 93, 113, 176].
- **Osmotic Drying:** Water is removed by submerging food in a hypertonic solution (high sugar/salt), using osmotic pressure to draw water out of the cells [Diagram of Osmotic Process: 112].
- **Intermediate Moisture Foods (IMF):** Foods that are semi-moist (like raisins) and stable without refrigeration because solutes are infused into them to lower water activity.

**Changes During Dehydration**

- **Physical:** Structural collapse of cells causes severe shrinkage and volume loss.
- **Browning:** Foods can experience enzymatic browning or non-enzymatic browning (Maillard reaction between amino acids and sugars, or caramelization of sugars).
- **Nutritional & Chemical:** High heat can lower the biological value of proteins, oxidize lipids (causing rancidity), and destroy heat-labile and water-soluble vitamins.

### II. Cold Preservation and Freezing

**Refrigeration (Cool Storage)**

- **Definition:** Storing food above its freezing point (usually 16°C down to -2°C). It slows down, but does not stop, microbial growth and chemical reactions.
- **Respiration Heat:** Fruits and vegetables continue to metabolize and respire in cold storage, giving off sensible heat that must be calculated into the refrigeration load to prevent them from rotting.
- **Chilling Injury:** Damage that occurs to certain living plant tissues (like bananas or tomatoes) when kept at temperatures that are too cold but not freezing.

**Freezing Principles and Thermodynamics**

- **Sensible vs. Latent Heat:** **Sensible heat** is heat removed that simply lowers the food's temperature. **Latent heat of fusion** is the massive amount of heat that must be removed to actually change the water from a liquid to solid ice at the freezing point.
- **Supercooling:** The phenomenon where the temperature of the food dips below its freezing point before any ice crystals actually form [Diagrams of Freezing Curve showing Supercooling: 21, 64, 65, 67, 74].
- **Crystallization:** Occurs in two steps. **Nucleation** is the formation of the first ice seed (either homogeneous or heterogeneous around a particle). **Crystal growth** is the enlargement of that nucleus.
- **Volume Changes:** Ice expands by 9% compared to liquid water, which can physically burst delicate cell walls.

**The Impact of Freezing Rate**

- **Slow Freezing:** Results in large ice crystals forming in the intercellular spaces. This crushes cell walls and violently dehydrates the cells as water migrates out to join the large crystals. Upon **thawing**, these ruptured cells leak their contents (drip loss) and lose their texture.
- **Fast Freezing:** Results in numerous tiny, microscopic ice crystals forming uniformly both inside and outside the cells. This causes minimal physical damage and preserves the original texture.
- **Freezer Burn:** A physical defect where food loses moisture via sublimation directly to the cold, dry freezer air, resulting in unsightly, dehydrated surfaces.

**Freezing Equipment and Methods**

- **Air Freezing:** Includes "sharp" freezing (still air) and **Blast Freezers**, which use high-velocity hurricane-force cold air to strip heat away quickly.
- **Indirect Contact Freezing:** Food is placed between metal plates or shelves that have refrigerant circulating through them. The plates press together to maximize contact (e.g., Pressure Plate Freezers) [Diagram of Plate Freezer: 35, 52, 53]. **Slush freezers** use a scraped-surface heat exchanger to partially freeze liquid foods.
- **Immersion Freezing:** Food is plunged directly into or sprayed with a low-freezing-point liquid like sodium chloride brine, glycerol, or propylene glycol.
- **Cryogenic Freezing:** Exposing food to liquefied gases with extremely low boiling points, primarily **Liquid Nitrogen (-196°C)** or carbon dioxide (-79°C). This causes near-instantaneous freezing with almost no tissue damage.

### III. Thermal Processing

**Degrees of Preservation**

- **Sterilization:** The complete destruction of all microorganisms, usually requiring 121°C for 15-30 minutes under pressure.
- **Commercial Sterility:** The heat treatment used for canned foods. It destroys pathogens and spoilage organisms that could grow under normal shelf storage, though a few highly heat-resistant (but harmless) spores might survive.
- **Pasteurization:** A milder heat treatment (<100°C) aimed primarily at destroying public health pathogens and extending shelf life by reducing the total microbial load.
- **Blanching:** A brief heat treatment (usually 2-10 minutes) used primarily to deactivate tissue enzymes before freezing or canning.

**Thermal Death Kinetics (Microbiology)**

- **D-value (Decimal Reduction Time):** The time in minutes at a specific temperature required to kill 90% of the bacterial population (dropping the count by one log cycle).
- **z-value:** The number of degrees (temperature) required to change the D-value by a factor of 10.
- **F-value (Lethality):** The total time in minutes required to kill a specified number of organisms. The standard is the $F_o$ value, which is the time at 121°C (250°F) assuming a z-value of 10°C.
- **Margin of Safety (12 D Concept):** In low-acid foods (pH > 4.6), the target pathogen is _Clostridium botulinum_. The industry standard requires a heat treatment severe enough to reduce the spore population by 12 D-values (a 12-log reduction).
- **Thermal Death Curves:** Graphs showing the logarithmic destruction of bacteria [Diagrams of Thermal Death Curves: 128, 129, 130, 131].

**Heat Transfer Mechanisms**

- **Conduction:** Heat moves molecule by molecule from the outside in. This occurs in solid foods (like canned meat or pumpkin puree).
- **Convection:** Heat transfer via currents in a fluid. This occurs in liquid foods (like broth or juice).
- **Cold Point:** The absolute slowest heating spot in a container. For conduction (solid food), it is the exact geographic center. For convection (liquid food), it is below the center due to rising hot currents [Diagrams of Thermocouple placement at the Cold Point: 133, 134].

**Thermal Processing Equipment**

- **Still Retorts:** Essentially large pressure cookers where cans are heated in batches by steam.
- **Agitating Retorts:** Retorts that physically shake or spin the cans during heating. This forces convection inside the can, drastically reducing processing time and improving food quality [Diagram of Agitating Retort: 140, 141].
- **Hydrostatic Cookers:** Continuous processing machines that use massive columns of water to act as a pressure seal against the central steam chamber [Diagram of Hydrostatic Cooker: 142, 143].
- **HTST (High-Temperature-Short-Time):** Used for liquids like milk. Fluid is pumped through a plate heat exchanger to reach a high temperature (e.g., 72°C) for a very short holding time (e.g., 15 seconds) [Diagrams of HTST Pasteurization: 144, 161, 162].
- **Aseptic Packaging:** The food and the packaging material are sterilized independently, then brought together and sealed inside a completely sterile environment.
- **Hot Pack/Hot Fill:** Pasteurized, highly acidic foods are filled into clean containers while still piping hot, effectively sterilizing the container itself.

**Changes in Food Due to Heat Processing**

- **Proteins:** Undergo denaturation (breaking of structures) and coagulation (solidifying, like eggs). Heat can destroy amino acids like lysine but broadly improves protein digestibility.
- **Carbohydrates:** Starches undergo gelatinization (swelling and absorbing water), and cell wall pectins break down, softening the food.
- **Fats & Lipids:** High heat can cause oxidative rancidity and flavor reversion.
- **Color & Flavor:** Volatile flavor compounds are degraded. Green chlorophyll breaks down to a yellow-brown, and anthocyanins fade. Browning occurs due to Maillard reactions and caramelization.
- **Nutritional Quality:** Heat-labile vitamins (like Vitamin C and B-complex) are destroyed. However, heat also has positive effects, such as destroying anti-nutritional enzymes and improving the bioavailability of nutrients like lycopene.
