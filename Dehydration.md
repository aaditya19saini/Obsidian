# Notes: Food Dehydration Technology & Drying Methods

## Principles of Food Dehydration Technology

**Definition:** Dehydration is the controlled, artificial removal of water from a food product under controlled conditions of temperature, humidity, and airflow.

**Explanation:** The primary goal is to lower the water activity ($a_w$) to a level where microbial growth and enzymatic reactions are inhibited. This involves simultaneous **heat transfer** (heat moving from the air into the food to vaporize water) and **mass transfer** (water vapor moving from the interior of the food to the surface, and then into the surrounding air).

**Key Details:**

- Extends shelf life significantly and reduces product weight/volume for easier transport.
    
- Differs from "sun drying" as dehydration relies on mechanically controlled, artificial heat and airflow.
    
- The efficiency depends on the surface area of the food, temperature, air velocity, and the relative humidity of the drying air.
    

**Example:** Converting fresh milk into milk powder to extend its shelf life from days to months without refrigeration.

## Mechanism of Drying: The Drying Rate Curve

**Definition:** A graphical representation of how the moisture content of a food changes over time during the dehydration process.

**Explanation:** Drying does not occur at a single, steady speed. The curve is divided into distinct periods based on where the water is being removed from.

**Key Details:**

- **Pre-heating Period:** The food warms up to the drying temperature; minimal drying occurs.
    
- **Constant Rate Period:** Moisture from the surface of the food evaporates rapidly. The rate of drying is limited only by the surrounding air conditions (temperature/humidity). Water diffuses from the inside to the surface as fast as it evaporates.
    
- **Falling Rate Period (1 & 2):** Surface moisture is gone. The drying rate slows down because it is now limited by how fast internal moisture can diffuse through the cellular structure to the surface. Most heat damage occurs here.
    

**Example:** When drying a sliced apple, the wet surface dries quickly (constant rate), but extracting the last bit of moisture from the center takes much longer (falling rate).

## Cabinet / Tray Drying

**Definition:** A batch drying method where food is spread on mesh trays inside an insulated cabinet, and hot air is circulated across them.

**Explanation:** Air is heated by coils and blown by fans horizontally across or vertically through the trays. Exhaust air is vented out to remove the moisture.

**Key Details:**

- Typically operates at 60-80°C.
    
- Low capital cost and flexible, but slow and labor-intensive.
    
- Often suffers from uneven drying; trays must be manually rotated.
    
- Suitable for fruits, vegetables, and herbs in small to medium batches.
    

## Fluidized Bed Drying

**Definition:** A continuous or batch method where high-velocity hot air is blown upward through a perforated plate, suspending food particles in the air stream.

**Explanation:** The upward force of the air equals the gravitational force on the food, causing the particles to act like a boiling fluid. This maximizes the surface area exposed to the hot air.

**Key Details:**

- Extremely efficient heat and mass transfer.
    
- Results in very uniform drying.
    
- Limited to small, particulate foods (e.g., peas, diced carrots, grains) that won't stick together.
    

## Drum Drying

**Definition:** A continuous drying method where a liquid, slurry, or paste is applied as a thin film onto the outer surface of a slowly rotating, steam-heated metal drum.

**Explanation:** As the drum rotates, the intense conductive heat boils off the water almost immediately. A stationary "doctor blade" scrapes the dried film off the drum before it completes a full rotation.

**Key Details:**

- High thermal efficiency but causes severe heat damage (cooked flavors, protein denaturation).
    
- Poor retention of vitamins and colors compared to other methods.
    
- Highly suitable for thick pastes and purees.
    

**Example:** Producing instant mashed potato flakes or cheap baby food cereals.

## Spray Drying

**Definition:** A rapid drying process that transforms a liquid or slurry into a dry powder by atomizing it into a hot gas stream.

**Explanation:** The liquid is pumped through a nozzle or spinning disc (atomizer) to create millions of tiny droplets. These droplets are exposed to hot air (150-300°C) inside a large chamber. The massive surface area causes instantaneous evaporation, cooling the air and protecting the food from heat damage.

**Key Details:**

- Very fast drying time (seconds).
    
- Produces highly soluble, free-flowing powders.
    
- Requires the feed to be a pumpable liquid.
    
- Used extensively for milk powder, instant coffee, and whey protein.
    

## Vacuum Drying

**Definition:** A method where food is dried inside a sealed chamber under significantly reduced atmospheric pressure.

**Explanation:** Lowering the pressure dramatically lowers the boiling point of water (e.g., water can boil at 30-40°C under a strong vacuum). This allows for rapid evaporation without exposing the food to destructive high temperatures.

**Key Details:**

- Ideal for highly heat-sensitive or easily oxidizable foods.
    
- High capital and operating costs due to the vacuum pumps and heavy pressure vessels.
    
- Prevents lipid oxidation because oxygen is removed from the chamber.
    

## Freeze Drying (Lyophilization)

**Definition:** A premium dehydration process where the food is first frozen solid, and then the ice is removed directly as vapor via sublimation under a high vacuum.

**Explanation:** The food never enters the liquid phase during drying. Gentle radiant heat is applied under a vacuum, causing the ice crystals to turn directly into gas.

**Key Details:**

- **Zero shrinkage**: The food retains its original size, shape, and porous cellular structure.
    
- Highest retention of flavor, color, and nutritional value (heat-labile vitamins are preserved).
    
- Excellent rehydration capacity.
    
- The most expensive and time-consuming commercial drying method.
    

**Example:** Premium instant coffee granules or dried strawberries in high-end breakfast cereals.

## Foam Mat Drying

**Definition:** A method where a liquid food is whipped into a stable foam using a foaming agent, spread onto a mat or belt, and dried with hot air.

**Explanation:** Whipping the liquid creates a massive internal surface area (the bubbles). When hot air hits the foam, moisture escapes rapidly through the thin bubble walls.

**Key Details:**

- Faster than standard tray drying and uses lower temperatures than drum drying.
    
- The resulting dried foam is highly porous and easily crushed into a soluble powder.
    
- Ideal for sticky or heat-sensitive fruit juices (e.g., mango, tomato, orange).

### Cover But Don't Stress

**Drying Methods Overview** _(Not yet asked but in syllabus — light coverage)_

- **Drum drying:** Uses steam-heated rotating cylinders; great for pastes/purees but causes high thermal degradation.
    
- **Foam mat drying:** Liquid whipped into foam for higher surface area; good for sticky fruit juices.
    
- **Freeze drying (Lyophilization):** Freezing followed by sublimation under vacuum; zero shrinkage, highest quality, highest cost.
    
- **Vacuum drying:** Reduced pressure lowers water's boiling point, allowing drying at low temperatures; ideal for heat-sensitive/oxidizable foods.
    
- **Fluidized bed drying:** High-velocity air suspends particulates; excellent for uniform drying of small items like peas.
