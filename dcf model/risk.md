When we talk about the **total risk of a business**, we are looking at the overall uncertainty that a company will not perform as expected, fail to meet its financial obligations, or generate lower returns than anticipated for its investors.

In corporate finance and asset pricing, total risk is mathematically represented by the **volatility (standard deviation) of a company's stock returns**. It is structurally divided into two major components: **Systematic Risk** and **Unsystematic Risk**.

### 1. Unsystematic Risk (Company-Specific Risk)

This represents the hazards that are unique to a specific business or its narrow industry. Because these risks are isolated to a single company, investors can largely eliminate them by building a diversified portfolio (investing in many different companies across various sectors).

Unsystematic risk is typically broken down into two main categories:

- **Business Risk:** The underlying uncertainty regarding a company's ability to generate operating profit (EBIT) from its core operations. It is driven by factors like:
    
    - _Revenue Volatility:_ Fluctuations in demand for the company’s products or services.
        
    - _Input Costs:_ Unexpected spikes in the price of raw materials or labor.
        
    - _Operational Failures:_ Supply chain disruptions, factory breakdowns, or poor management decisions.
        
- **Financial Risk:** The additional risk placed on common shareholders when a company chooses to fund its operations using debt instead of equity.
    
    - If a business relies heavily on loans, it takes on fixed interest payments. If cash flow drops, the company still must pay its lenders, dramatically increasing the probability of bankruptcy or financial distress.
        

### 2. Systematic Risk (Market Risk)

This refers to the macroeconomic hazards that affect the _entire_ market simultaneously. It cannot be diversified away, no matter how many different stocks you own. Examples include:

- Changes in interest rates or central bank policies.
    
- Broad economic recessions or spikes in inflation.
    
- Geopolitical instability, wars, or global pandemics.
    

In finance, systematic risk is measured by **Beta ($\beta$)**, which gauges how sensitive a specific stock's price is relative to movements in the broader market.

### How Total Risk Ties into WACC and Your Model

In your financial primer, it is noted that the **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)** measures the "total risk of the business" from the perspective of its capital providers.

When setting up your discount rate ($r$) for a DCF or Reverse DCF model:

1. **For Debt ($R_d$):** Lenders look at both **Business Risk** and **Financial Risk** to determine creditworthiness. If a company has volatile operations or a massive pile of existing debt, banks charge a higher interest rate to compensate for that company-specific risk.
    
2. **For Equity ($R_e$):** According to modern portfolio theory (specifically the Capital Asset Pricing Model, or CAPM), public equity investors assume they can diversify away unsystematic risk. Therefore, they only demand to be compensated for **Systematic Risk ($\beta$)**.
    

When you blend these together into WACC, it functions as a comprehensive hurdle rate reflecting the blended operational and macroeconomic risk profile of the firm. A high-risk biotech startup will have an elevated WACC because its future outcomes are highly uncertain, causing future cash flows to be aggressively discounted.
