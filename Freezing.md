

# Principles of Freezing

**Definition:** **Freezing** is a preservation method that extends shelf life by lowering the temperature of food below its freezing point, causing water to phase-change into ice.

**Explanation:** By converting liquid water into solid ice, freezing reduces the **water activity ($a_w$)** of the food. This simultaneously removes the moisture required for microbial growth and significantly slows down the rate of chemical and enzymatic degradation reactions.

**Key Details:**

- Standard commercial frozen storage temperature is typically **-18°C** or below.
    
- It does not completely destroy pathogenic or spoilage microorganisms; it merely arrests their multiplication.
    
- Solutes in the food cause **freezing point depression**, meaning the food freezes at a temperature lower than pure water (0°C).
    

**Example:** Storing peas at -18°C prevents spoilage bacteria from growing and halts enzymatic softening for several months.

**Definition:** The **freezing curve** is a time-temperature graph that plots the physical cooling and phase change of a food product as heat is removed.

**Explanation:** The curve visually represents the removal of heat in distinct stages. It shows the temperature dropping, dipping slightly below the freezing point without freezing, flattening out as water turns to ice, and then dropping again.

**Key Details:**

- **Stage 1 (Pre-cooling):** Removal of **sensible heat**. Temperature drops from ambient down to the freezing point.
    
- **Stage 2 (Supercooling):** Temperature dips slightly below the freezing point before ice crystals begin to form.
    
- **Stage 3 (Nucleation & Phase Change):** The temperature rises slightly to the freezing point and remains relatively flat (the freezing plateau). Here, **latent heat of crystallization** is removed as liquid water turns to solid ice.
    
- **Stage 4 (Post-cooling):** Once mostly frozen, sensible heat is removed from the frozen matrix, lowering the food's temperature to the storage temperature (e.g., -18°C).
    

**Example:** When freezing a strawberry, the temperature drops steadily to 0°C, pauses while the internal water turns to ice, and then rapidly drops to the freezer temperature.

## Ice Crystal Formation: Fast vs. Slow Freezing

**Definition:** The rate at which heat is removed dictates the size and location of ice crystals formed within the food tissue.

**Explanation:** The quality of frozen food is highly dependent on crystal size. Fast freezing rapidly pushes the food through the zone of maximum ice crystal formation, creating many tiny crystals. Slow freezing lingers in this zone, allowing water to migrate and form large, damaging crystals.

**Key Details:**

- **Slow Freezing:** Produces few, very large ice crystals. These form extracellularly (outside cells), puncturing cell walls and causing massive **drip loss** (loss of moisture and nutrients) upon thawing.
    
- **Fast Freezing:** Produces numerous, small, evenly distributed intracellular and extracellular ice crystals. This preserves cellular structure and texture.
    

**Example:** Commercially blast-frozen fish fillets retain their firm texture, whereas a fish frozen slowly in a home freezer becomes mushy and leaks fluid when thawed.

## Air Freezing Methods

**Definition:** Freezing systems that use cold air as the heat exchange medium to freeze food products.

**Explanation:** Cold air is circulated around the product to remove heat. The efficiency depends on air temperature and air velocity.

**Key Details:**

- **Still Air Freezing / Sharp Freezing:** Uses practically still air. It is a slow batch process with poor heat transfer, largely obsolete for commercial freezing but common in domestic freezers.
    
- **Air Blast Freezing:** Uses high-velocity cold air (often -30°C to -40°C) circulated by fans. It is much faster and can be a batch (cabinet) or continuous (tunnel/belt) process.
    
- **Fluidized Bed Freezing:** Cold air is blown upward through a mesh belt at a velocity high enough to suspend the food particles in the cold air stream.
    
- Fluidized beds are used to produce **IQF (Individually Quick Frozen)** products.
    

**Example:** Fluidized bed freezing is used for green peas, ensuring they freeze rapidly and do not clump together in the bag.

## Indirect Contact Freezing (Plate Freezers)

**Definition:** A method where food is pressed between hollow, refrigerated metal plates containing circulating refrigerant.

**Explanation:** Heat transfers via conduction directly from the food (or its packaging) to the cold metal plates. Physical pressure is applied to ensure maximum surface contact and eliminate air pockets.

**Key Details:**

- Highly efficient due to direct conductive heat transfer.
    
- Restricted to products with regular, flat shapes or products pressed into rectangular cartons.
    
- Operates as a batch process using horizontal or vertical plate configurations.
    

**Example:** Producing rectangular blocks of frozen spinach or fish fillets.

## Cryogenic Freezing

**Definition:** The ultra-fast freezing of food achieved by direct contact with liquefied or solidified gases (cryogens).

**Explanation:** Cryogens absorb massive amounts of heat as they undergo a phase change (liquid to gas, or solid to gas) directly on the surface of the food, resulting in nearly instantaneous freezing.

**Key Details:**

- **Liquid Nitrogen ($LN_2$):** Boils at **-196°C**. Sprayed directly onto the food in a tunnel freezer.
    
- **Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$):** Used as a sublimating solid (dry ice) at **-78°C** or sprayed as a liquid that expands into snow.
    
- **Advantages:** Unmatched freezing speed, smallest possible ice crystals, minimal dehydration loss, compact equipment, and exclusion of oxygen.
    
- **Disadvantages:** Extremely high operating cost due to the continuous consumption of refrigerants.
    

**Example:** High-value products like delicate berries, shrimp, or premium burger patties are often cryogenically frozen to perfectly preserve texture.

## Cold Storage Requirements & Refrigeration Load

**Definition:** The operational parameters and calculations required to maintain frozen foods safely over time.

**Explanation:** Proper cold storage requires holding food at a constant, uniform temperature while accounting for all sources of heat entering the cold room.

**Key Details:**

- **Refrigeration Load:** The total amount of heat that the refrigeration equipment must remove. It includes the heat of the product, heat transmission through walls/insulation, air infiltration from doors, and heat generated by equipment/personnel.
    
- Temperature fluctuations must be strictly avoided to prevent quality degradation.
    
- Adequate air circulation is required to maintain uniform temperatures, but too much velocity can cause surface dehydration.
    

**Example:** Sizing a compressor for a cold storage warehouse requires calculating the refrigeration load based on the tons of frozen goods arriving daily and the ambient summer temperatures outside.

## Quality Changes and Defects in Frozen Storage

**Definition:** Physical and chemical deteriorations that occur when frozen food is improperly packaged or subjected to temperature fluctuations during storage.

**Explanation:** Even at -18°C, certain physical phenomena and slow chemical reactions can degrade food quality, primarily affecting moisture content, texture, and cellular integrity.

**Key Details:**

- **Freezer Burn:** Severe surface dehydration caused by the sublimation of ice directly into vapor. It results in dry, discolored, and tough patches on the food surface. Prevented by moisture-vapor-proof packaging.
    
- **Recrystallization:** When storage temperatures fluctuate, small ice crystals melt and refreeze onto larger ones. Over time, this degrades the texture of fast-frozen foods, mimicking the damage of slow freezing.
    
- **Chill Injury:** A physiological disorder occurring in certain susceptible fruits and vegetables (like bananas or tomatoes) when exposed to cold, refrigeration temperatures (above freezing). It causes surface pitting and internal discoloration.
    

**Example:** Leaving a poorly wrapped steak in the freezer for a year will result in freezer burn, turning the edges gray and leathery due to localized dehydration.

### High Priority Topics ⚑

**Freezing — the freezing curve, air freezing methods, cryogenic freezing, advantages and disadvantages** _(examined previously — high repeat likelihood)_

- **Freezing Curve:** Be able to describe the stages: removal of sensible heat (pre-cooling), supercooling (dipping below freezing point), nucleation and removal of latent heat (the freezing plateau), and post-cooling.
    
- **Fast vs. Slow Freezing:** Fast freezing forms many small intracellular crystals (preserves texture); slow freezing forms large extracellular crystals that puncture cell walls, causing drip loss.
    
- **Air Freezing:** Fluidized bed freezing utilizes high-velocity upward air to suspend and freeze particulate foods, resulting in Individually Quick Frozen (IQF) products.
    
- **Cryogenic Freezing:** Uses Liquid Nitrogen (-196°C) or Carbon Dioxide (-78°C) in direct contact with food. _Advantage:_ Extremely rapid freezing, minimal cellular damage, no dehydration. _Disadvantage:_ Very high cost of consumable refrigerants.
    

**Cold storage requirements** _(examined previously — high repeat likelihood)_

- Requires maintaining a strict, constant temperature (typically -18°C or below).
    
- Temperature fluctuations must be avoided to prevent recrystallization of ice.
    
- Proper calculation of the **refrigeration load** (product heat, wall transmission, door infiltration) is required to maintain the environment.
    

### Medium Priority Topics

**Short definitions: chill injury, refrigeration load, freezer burn** _(appeared once — worth covering)_

- **Refrigeration load:** The total quantity of heat that must be removed from a cold storage space, including product heat, wall heat transfer, and infiltration.
    
- **Chill injury:** Physiological damage to susceptible produce (like tomatoes or bananas) caused by exposure to cold but non-freezing temperatures, resulting in pitting and discoloration.
