# 1. The Core Concept: Biotransformation

- Metabolism, also known as biotransformation, is the conversion of a drug from one chemical form to another.

- This is fundamentally a detoxification process and a key method of drug elimination from the body.

- The duration and intensity of a drug's action depend on its rate of removal.

- **The Lipid-Water Paradox:** ==Drugs (xenobiotics) must be lipid-soluble to be effectively absorbed. However, lipid-soluble molecules bypass kidney excretion.== Therefore, **metabolism is required to convert these lipid-soluble drugs into water-soluble metabolites so the kidneys can easily excrete them.**


### 2. Outcomes of Drug Metabolism

Metabolism doesn't always just "turn off" a drug. It can result in several different activity states:

- **Pharmacological Inactivation:** The drug becomes an **inactive metabolite** (e.g., Salicylic acid converting to Salicyluric acid).

- **Equal Activity:** The ==metabolite is just as active as the parent drug== (e.g., Codeine converting to Morphine).

- **Toxicological Activation:** The drug is **converted into a toxic metabolite** (e.g., Paracetamol converting to an Imidoquinone metabolite).

- **Activation of Inactive Drugs:** A "prodrug" is activated by metabolism (e.g., Chloramphenicol palmitate converting to active Chloramphenicol).


### 3. Where Does Metabolism Happen?

- **The Liver:** The liver is the undisputed major organ for drug biotransformation (hepatic metabolism).
    
- **Extrahepatic Metabolism:** Other organs also pitch in. The order of metabolizing capacity outside the liver is:

```
  Liver> Lungs > Kidneys > Intestine > Placenta > Adrenals > Skin.
```
    

### 4. The Enzymatic Workforce

The actual chemical conversions are handled by specific enzymes:

- **Microsomal Enzymes:** These catalyze the majority of drug biotransformation reactions. 
- They are derived from the r**ough endoplasmic reticulum** and ==show a selectivity towards lipid-soluble substrates==.
- They catalyze **oxidative, reductive, hydrolytic, and glucuronidation reactions*
- Examples include ==monooxygenases, cytochrome P450, and glucuronyl transferase.==



- **Non-Microsomal Enzymes:** These are present in a ==soluble form within the cytoplasm==. 
- They are non-specific and **act on relatively water-soluble xenobiotics**, catalyzing some ==oxidative, reductive, hydrolytic, and conjugation reactions.==
- Examples include oxidases and peroxidases.


### 5. Chemical Pathways: Phase I and Phase II

![[Pasted image 20260312191526.png]]


The overall flow converts a lipid-soluble drug into a Phase I metabolite, which then undergoes Phase II reactions to become a highly water-soluble metabolite.

- **Phase I Reactions (Functionalization/Asynthetic):**
    
    - Include ==oxidative, reductive, and hydrolytic reactions==.
        
    - They work by ==adding or unmasking a polar== functional group (like -OH, -COOH, -NH2, or -SH).
        
    - During this phase, <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">pharmacological activity may be lost</mark>, but the polarity of the molecule might not yet be increased enough for excretion.
        
- **Phase II Reactions (Conjugation/Synthetic):**
    
    - These involve the ==covalent attachment of small, polar endogenous molecules== (like<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;"> glucuronic acid, sulfate, or glycine</mark>) to the Phase I metabolite.
        
    - This forms **highly polar, water-soluble conjugates.**
        
    - This phase results in a <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">total loss of pharmacologic activity</mark>, serving as the "true" detoxification reactions, and heavily involves transferase enzymes.
        

### 6. Specific Reaction Types

- **Oxidation:** Includes side chain hydroxylation, aromatic hydroxylation, deamination, and N, O, S dealkylation.
    
- **Reduction:** Involves the reduction of nitro and nitroso groups, azo groups, and certain aldehydes.
    
- **Hydrolysis:** Carried out by enzymes like cholinesterases, phosphatases, and sulfatases acting on esters and amides.
    
- **Conjugation:** Includes esterification, amidation, mixed anhydride formation, hemiacetal formation, and etherization.

### 7. Factors Affecting Biotransformation

- The rate and extent of metabolism are governed by the drug's physicochemical properties.
    
- Chemical factors play a huge role, such as the induction or inhibition of drug-metabolizing enzymes and the presence of environmental chemicals.
    
- Biological factors also heavily influence the process, including species, strain, sex, and age.
