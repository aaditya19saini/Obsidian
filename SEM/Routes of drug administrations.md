### 1. Selecting a Route

The route of administration is simply the means by which a drug is introduced into the body. Selecting the correct route is critical because it dictates the rate and extent of drug absorption.

The criteria for selection include:

- The required type of action (<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">fast vs. slow</mark>).
- The <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">specific site of action.</mark>
- The physicochemical properties of the drug.
- The bioavailability of the drug via different routes.
- The accuracy of the required dose.
- The drug's stability in the gastrointestinal tract (GIT).
- The condition of the patient (e.g., are they conscious or vomiting?).


### 2. The Major Routes

**A. Topical Route**

- **Mechanism:** ==Action occurs underneath the point of application.== Effectiveness depends on the lipid solubility of the drug.
    
- **Use:** Convenient local application of powders, lotions, pastes, ointments, and plasters to the skin, eye, nose, ear, or throat.
    
- **Benefit:** Achieves high concentrations at the desired local site.
    

**B. Sublingual Route**

- **Mechanism:** The formulation is placed under the tongue to dissolve.
    
- **Benefits:** Provides fast action, avoids degradation in the GIT, and allows the drug to enter systemic circulation directly, bypassing portal circulation (the first-pass effect).
    
- **Limitation:** Only a few formulations can use this route (e.g., GTN, Buprenorphine).
    

**C. Oral Route (GIT)**

- **Benefits:** Safe, convenient, economical, non-invasive, painless, and very commonly used.
    
- **Disadvantages:**
    
    - Slow onset of action.
        
    - Difficult to achieve effective high plasma concentrations.
        
    - Subject to the first-pass effect (liver metabolism).
        
    - Variable absorption due to other factors.
        
    - Cannot be used for drugs unstable in the GIT, or for patients who are vomiting, unconscious, or uncooperative.
        
    - Can cause irritation to the gastric mucosa.
        

### 3. Parenteral Routes (Injections)

- **General Benefits:** Fast onset, useful in emergencies, avoids gastric destruction, avoids food interference, and can be used on unconscious or vomiting patients.
    
- **General Disadvantages:** Painful, not a "safe" route (once administered, it cannot be withdrawn), requires experts, is expensive, and requires strict sterility.
    

**Specific Parenteral Types:**

- **Intradermal (10-15° angle):** Administered into the dermis. Used only for specific purposes like BCG vaccines or sensitivity testing.
    
- **Subcutaneous (45° angle):** Administered into subcutaneous tissue. Limited to small volumes (1ml), but self-administration is possible. Can be used for suspensions and depot preparations (for prolonged action).
    
- **Intramuscular (90° angle):** Administered directly into skeletal muscles. Absorption is faster from aqueous solutions. Self-injection is not possible due to the deep penetration needed, but depot preparations and aqueous suspensions can be used.
    
- **Intravenous (25° angle):** Administered directly into the vein, offering very fast action, precise blood concentration maintenance, and 100% bioavailability. Can handle larger volumes (1-500ml), but volumes over 15ml must be isotonic with blood. Oily injections and suspensions cannot be used here.
    

### 4. Other Routes

- **Intrathecal:** Administered directly into the subarachnoid space surrounding the spinal cord (e.g., spinal anesthetics). Only highly lipid-soluble drugs will reach the brain from here.
    
- **Inhalation:** Delivered into the alveoli of the lungs, which offer a large surface area. Provides rapid onset and termination of action for vapors, aerosols, and gases.
    
- **Less Commonly Used:** Intracardiac, Rectal, Vaginal, and Transdermal.
