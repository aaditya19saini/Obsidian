Question 2: Describe the design and working of centrifugal filter
Answer: 
**Design** The structural design of this unit is straightforward. Its main component is a **perforated bowl** that spins on its axis. To act as a filter, the inner wall of this perforated bowl is lined with a porous filter medium, most commonly a **nylon filter bag**.

**Working Principle** The machine combines the principles of filtration with centrifugal force.

- A suspension or slurry is fed continuously into the center of the spinning basket.
- The rotational movement generates centrifugal force, which pushes the mixture outward against the walls of the bowl.
- The liquid portion is forced through the nylon filter bag and out through the perforations in the bowl.
- The solid particles are retained by the filter bag and accumulate to form a solid **filter cake** against the basket's inner surface.

**Operating Conditions and Applications** Because of its perforated design and filter bag, this type of centrifuge cannot withstand extremely high centrifugal forces. It typically operates at moderate speeds of up to **4000 rpm** and can handle continuous feed rates ranging from **50 to 300 L/min**.

Industrially, the basket centrifuge is particularly useful for separating larger, bulkier solids. Common applications include harvesting **fungal mycelia** (molds) from fermentation broths and recovering **crystalline compounds**.

Every choice made during upstream bioprocessing—from the selected microbial strain to the formulation of the media—directly impacts how easily and cost-effectively the final product can be recovered and purified.

Here is a detailed breakdown of how specific upstream factors influence downstream efficiency:

**1. Characteristics of Microbes** The physical properties of the producer microorganisms significantly dictate the complexity of the initial solid-liquid separation and disruption stages:

For instance, separating tiny bacteria (around 1 $\mu$m) is much harder than separating larger cell aggregates (up to 4000 $\mu$m). - **Cell Wall Rigidity:** For intracellular products, the rigidity of the cell wall directly impacts the energy and equipment required for the homogenization or cell disruption steps.
- **Flocculation Characteristics:** A microbe's natural ability to aggregate (flocculate) can drastically improve the efficiency of initial cell removal without the need for excessive chemical coagulants.

**2. Media Components and Fermentation By-Products** The broth that leaves the fermenter is a highly complex matrix, and what goes into the fermenter must eventually be removed:

question 4: A researcher using ammonium sulfate precipitation to isolate a target protein finds that at 30% saturation, the protein remains soluble, while at 60% saturation, the protein precipitates. Scientifically explain the reason for such an observation with suitable reasoning.

answer: This observation is a classic demonstration of **"salting-out,"** a physicochemical process where high salt concentrations drastically decrease protein solubility.

The scientific explanation for why the protein remains soluble at 30% but precipitates at 60% comes down to the solution's **ionic strength** and a severe competition for water molecules.

**At 30% Saturation (Protein Remains Soluble)** At this moderate salt concentration, the target protein still retains its protective **hydration shell**—a tightly bound layer of water molecules surrounding the protein's hydrophilic surface. This physical water barrier, combined with the repulsive forces of the protein's electrical double layer, prevents the individual protein molecules from interacting with one another. Because these repulsive forces remain intact, the protein stays safely dissolved in the liquid.

**At 60% Saturation (Protein Precipitates)** Ammonium sulfate is extremely effective at increasing a solution's ionic strength. When the saturation is pushed to 60%, the massive influx of salt ions heavily competes with the proteins for the available water molecules.

Because the salt ions have a higher affinity for water, they effectively pull the water away, **stripping the protein of its protective hydration shell**. Once this water barrier is removed, the previously hidden **hydrophobic zones** on the protein's surface become exposed to the environment. To minimize their contact with the surrounding liquid, these hydrophobic patches on neighboring proteins strongly attract one another. These attractive hydrophobic interactions overpower any remaining electrostatic repulsion, causing the naked protein molecules to aggregate and crash out of the solution as a solid precipitate.

**The Governing Equation** This phenomenon is mathematically defined by the **Cohn equation**: logS=β−KI (where S is protein solubility, I is ionic strength, and β and K are constants). The equation shows that a protein's solubility drops as ionic strength increases. In your researcher's case, moving from 30% to 60% saturation increased the ionic strength (I) past the specific solubility threshold of the target protein, forcing it to precipitate.

Question 5: Explain the differences in working principle of organic and inorganic flocculants with suitable examples.
Answer: While your query refers to "organic and inorganic flocculants," the provided sources specifically categorize these substances into **coagulating agents** (which are simple inorganic salts) and **flocculating agents** (which are organic polymers). Both are used to overcome the natural barriers—such as negative surface charges, hydrophilic water barriers, and steric hindrance—that normally prevent microbial cells from aggregating.

Here is the difference in their working principles:

**Inorganic Agents (Coagulants)**

- **Working Principle:** Coagulation is primarily a **chemical process**. Because microbial cells naturally carry a net negative surface charge, they electrostatically repel each other. Inorganic coagulants act as simple electrolytes that destabilize the particles through **charge neutralization**. By neutralizing the repulsive negative charges and reducing surface hydrophilicity, they allow the dispersed cells to come together and form small initial flocs.
- **Examples:** Alum, lime, and iron salts.

**Organic Agents (Flocculants)**

- **Working Principle:** Flocculation is a **physical phenomenon**. Organic flocculants are typically high molecular weight polymers or oligomers. Rather than just altering charges, these long polymer chains work via **polymer bridging**. They physically connect and entangle the smaller, coagulated flocs together, agglomerating them into much larger particles that can settle out of the liquid very easily.
- **Examples:** Polyacrylamide and guar gum.

Question 6: Describe the differences in working principle of various categories of detergents used in cell disruption. Cite one potential disadvantage linked with the use of detergents.
answer: Detergents used in cell disruption are categorized into three main types: anionic, non-ionic, and cationic. While they all generally function by directly damaging the lipoproteins of the microbial cell membrane to release intracellular contents, their specific working principles differ based on their chemical nature:

- **Anionic detergents** (such as sodium dodecyl sulfate, or SDS): These disrupt cells by reorganizing the cell membrane and disturbing protein-protein interactions.
- **Non-ionic detergents** (such as Triton X-100): These are milder in nature and function primarily by breaking lipid-lipid or lipid-protein interactions within the membrane.
- **Cationic detergents** (such as ethyl trimethyl ammonium bromide): These are speculated to act specifically on the lipopolysaccharides and phospholipids present in the cell membrane.

**Potential Disadvantage** A significant disadvantage linked with the use of detergents is that they can cause many target proteins to **denature**. Additionally, the presence of these chemicals can interfere with subsequent downstream purification steps, making the overall recovery process more difficult and limiting their practical application in large-scale industrial processes.
