### 1. The Core Concept of Excretion

- Excretion is the loss of the unchanged drug and its metabolites via secretory organs.
    
- These secretory organs eliminate polar (water-soluble) compounds much more efficiently than lipid-soluble ones.
    
- Therefore, lipid-soluble compounds must be metabolized into more polar compounds before they can be eliminated.
    
- The main routes of excretion are:
    
    1. Renal (kidneys) – the primary route.
        
    2. Biliary and fecal.
        
    3. Pulmonary (lungs) – specifically for gases and vapors like halothane and alcohol.
        
    4. Other minor routes like breast milk, saliva, sweat, tears, hair, and skin.
        

### 2. Renal Excretion (The Primary Route)

- The kidney is the most important excretory organ, and its basic functional unit is the nephron.
    
- A nephron consists of the glomerulus, proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, and collecting tubule.
    
- The overall rate of excretion is calculated as: Rate of excretion = Rate of filtration + Rate of secretion - Rate of reabsorption.
    

**A. Glomerular Filtration (GF)**

- This is a passive process driven by the hydrostatic pressure of the blood flowing through the capillaries.
    
- It filters most ionized and unionized compounds, but it cannot filter drugs bound to plasma proteins or blood cells.
    
- Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is the volume of fluid filtered into the Bowman's capsule per unit of time.
    
- Creatinine and inulin are used as diagnostic indicators for kidney function because they are completely removed by GF and are neither actively secreted nor reabsorbed.
    

**B. Active Tubular Secretion**

- This is a carrier-mediated process that requires energy to transport compounds against a concentration gradient.
    
- It primarily occurs in the proximal tubule and is responsible for excreting 80% of the drug delivered to the kidney.
    
- It utilizes two specific carrier systems: an acid carrier system (for drugs like penicillins and salicylic acid) and a base carrier system (for drugs like morphine and dopamine).
    
- Because it relies on carriers, drugs can compete for these pathways, leading to drug-drug interactions (e.g., Probenecid retards the excretion of penicillin).
    

**C. Tubular Reabsorption**

- This occurs after filtration, takes place all along the renal tubule, and results in an increased half-life for the drug.
    
- **Passive reabsorption:** Rapidly reabsorbs lipid-soluble drugs based on concentration gradients and pKa; it is highly dependent on urine pH.
    
- **Active reabsorption:** Conserves necessary endogenous substances (electrolytes, glucose, vitamins) and actively reabsorbs compounds like lithium, fluoride, and uric acid.
    

### 3. Factors Affecting Renal Excretion

- The physicochemical properties of the drug and its plasma concentration.
    
- Alteration of urine pH and diuresis (e.g., making urine alkaline helps treat phenobarbital intoxication).
    
- The drug's distribution and protein binding status.
    
- Age (GFR declines slowly as we age) and underlying disease states.
    

### 4. Biliary, Fecal, and Other Routes

- **Biliary & Fecal:** Handles orally unabsorbed drugs and metabolites excreted in the bile. Molecular weight and polarity determine biliary excretion, and conjugation enhances it.
    
- **Enterohepatic Circulation (EHC):** Some drug conjugates in the bile are dumped into the intestine, hydrolyzed back into the parent drug, and reabsorbed into the blood. This significantly prolongs the drug's half-life.
    
- **Breast Milk:** Milk is more acidic than plasma (pH 6.6), causing basic drugs to get trapped; hence, drugs should generally be avoided during lactation.
    
- **Saliva:** Also more acidic than plasma (trapping basic drugs), but drug concentration here parallels plasma, making it useful for bioavailability testing.
    
- **Hair & Skin:** While quantitatively unimportant for overall excretion, detecting toxic metals here has major forensic significance.
    

### 5. The Concept of Clearance

- Clearance measures kidney function and is defined as the hypothetical volume of body fluids from which a drug is completely removed in a specific time period ($ml/min$).
    
- $Total~body~clearance = Renal~clearance + Non~renal~clearance$.
    
- Renal clearance ($Cl_{R}$) specifically measures the volume of plasma cleared of unchanged drug by the kidneys per unit time.
    

**Renal Clearance Equations:**

- $Cl_{R} = \frac{Rate~of~urinary~excretion}{Plasma~drug~concentration}$.
    
- $Cl_{R} = \frac{Rate~of~filtration + Rate~of~secretion - Rate~of~reabsorption}{Plasma~drug~concentration}$.
