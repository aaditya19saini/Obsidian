### 1. The Basics of Drug Distribution

- Distribution is defined as the reversible transfer of a drug between one compartment and another, specifically between the blood and the extravascular fluids and tissues.
    
- It is a **passive process.**
    
- A drug's onset of action, intensity, and duration of action all depend heavily on its distribution.
    
- Importantly, the distribution of a drug is not uniform throughout the body.
    

### 2. Factors Governing Drug Distribution

The way a drug spreads through the body is influenced by several key factors:

**A. Tissue Permeability & Physicochemical Properties**

- A drug's ability to permeate tissue is affected by its **molecular size, lipid solubility, degree of ionization, and partition coefficient.**
    
- Drugs must navigate physiological barriers, including the ==simple capillary endothelial barrier, simple cell membrane barrier, cerebrospinal fluid barrier, blood-brain barrier, and placental barrier.
    

**B. Organ/Tissue Size and Perfusion Rate**

- Perfusion rate is defined as the volume of blood that flows per unit of time per unit volume of the tissue, usually measured in $ml/min/ml$.
    
- Organs are categorized by their blood flow: highly perfused, moderately perfused, or poorly perfused.
    
- The relationship between perfusion and distribution is defined by the equations:
    
    $K_{t} = \frac{Perfusion~rate}{K_{t/b}}$. Distribution half life $= \frac{0.693}{K_{t}} = \frac{0.693K_{t/b}}{Perfusion~rate}$.


### 3. Apparent Volume of Distribution ($V_{d}$)

- Once a drug is distributed and equilibrium is achieved, different organs and tissues will hold different concentrations of that drug.
    
- The mathematical relationship is expressed as $X = V_{d}C$.
    
- In this equation, $X$ is the amount of drug in the body, $C$ is the concentration of the drug in the plasma, and $V_{d}$ is the apparent volume of distribution.
    
- To give you a baseline, the real volume of distribution for Total Body Water (TBW) is $42$ liters, broken down into: vascular fluid/blood ($6$ liters, with $3$ being plasma), Extracellular Fluid or ECF ($12$ liters), and Intracellular Fluid or ICF ($24$ liters).
    
- **Impact of Binding on $V_{d}$:** * ==Drugs that selectively bind to plasma proteins or other blood components end up with a smaller apparent $V_{d}$ than their true $V_{d}$== (e.g., **Warfarin has a $V_{d}$ of $10$ liters**).
    
    - ==Drugs that bind strongly to extravascular tissues will have an apparent $V_{d}$ that is larger than their true $V_{d}$== (e.g., Chloroquine has an immense $V_{d}$ of $1500$ liters).


### 4. Protein Binding of Drugs

- Drugs commonly interact and bind with blood and extravascular tissue components.
    
- This binding involves **weak chemical bonds**, such as ==hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic bonds, ionic bonds, or Van der Waals forces.==
    
- The basic equation is: ==Free drug + Protein $\rightleftharpoons$ D-Protein complex.==
    
- **Characteristics of a Bound Drug----->Protein:** A bound drug becomes ==pharmacokinetically and pharmacodynamically inert.== This means it is **neither metabolized, excreted, nor pharmacologically active while bound.**
    
- **Blood Component Binding:** Drugs can bind to ==human serum albumin, glycoproteins, lipoproteins, globulins, and hemoglobin==. The text notes that **plasma protein binding decreases the half-life of drugs.**
    
- **Extravascular Tissue Binding:** Drugs can bind to ==fats, bones, and other tissues==. This causes the drug to localize at a particular site, increases the apparent $V_{d}$, and increases the drug's biological half-life. Generally, a bound drug's half-life is increased.
    

### 5. Overall Significance of Protein/Tissue Binding

- Binding ultimately impacts a drug's absorption, systemic solubility, and overall distribution.
    
- It dictates tissue binding, apparent $V_{d}$, and how the drug is stored in the body.
    
- It can also lead to displacement interactions and potential toxicity
